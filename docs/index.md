## **Article #1 - AI for Networking Has Entered Its Next Chapter** 
By: Jeramiah Pauly, Associate Account Manager - Upstate NY Region

<div class="article-section" markdown>

<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-style:italic; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  Meet Arista AVA
</p>

As we head into the new year, many of the conversations I am having across Upstate New York sound the same.

- Networks are more critical than ever.
- Teams are leaner than ever.
- And complexity keeps creeping up.

That is exactly why I am excited to share what Arista has been building behind the scenes. Arista AVA: AI for Networking.

AVA is not a bolt on feature or another dashboard. It represents a fundamental shift in how networks can be operated, understood, and improved over time.

“*What’s exciting is the shift from static dashboards to an AI agent that understands network state. Operators can now communicate with their data, gain a 360-degree view of the infrastructure, and collaborate with the NOC to troubleshoot issues or plan new deployments. This represents a major step forward not just for Arista, but for the networking industry as a whole.*” - [Nick Lippis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h2zMyqwUMF0), Founder of ONUG

<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  What AVA Actually Is
</p>

AVA stands for Autonomous Virtual Assistant, and that distinction matters.

AVA is not just another large language model answering questions based on public information or generic training data. It is a true assistant that understands the unique context of your environment.

AVA knows your network. Every device. Every configuration. Every dependency. Every event.

Instead of searching the internet for possible explanations, AVA reasons directly from the live and historical data of your infrastructure. That is what allows it to deliver answers that are relevant, accurate, and actionable.

<figure markdown>
![Image Placement](img/Jan2026-AVA-1.png){: style="height:375px;width:800px"} 
    <figcaption>  </figcaption>
</figure>


<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  From Reactive Operations to Autonomous Intelligence
</p>

AVA delivers a truly unified agentic AI framework for networking, establishing the foundation for autonomous, self-driving networks.

What does that actually mean in practice?

Instead of reacting to alerts after users complain, AVA continuously monitors the environment, understands context across the entire network, and proactively identifies issues before they become outages. This dramatically simplifies day to day operations and transforms AIOps from a buzzword into something that actually helps engineers do their jobs better.

<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  Built on EOS. Powered by Real Data.
</p>

AVA is powered by Arista EOS, which provides the open and programmable interface AI agents need to interact directly with the network in a meaningful way.

At the center of it all is NetDL, Arista’s comprehensive data lake. NetDL aggregates all relevant network and operational data, including telemetry from wired, wireless, data center, security, and even third party systems.

The result is a single source of truth, not fragmented views or siloed tools.

This unified data foundation gives AVA the full context it needs to reason, correlate, and act intelligently across the entire infrastructure.

<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  Open by Design. Ready for the Future.
</p>

One of the most important aspects of AVA is openness.

Through support for Model Context Protocol, AVA allows AI agents from Arista or trusted third parties to securely access the same shared context. This means customers are not locked into a closed system, and innovation can extend well beyond a single vendor.

It is a future proof approach that aligns perfectly with how modern IT teams want to operate.

<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  What AVA Enables Today
</p>

With this foundation in place, AVA unlocks powerful new capabilities, including:

- Multi domain event correlation
    - AVA correlates events across wired, wireless, data center, and security domains to identify a single root cause instead of flooding teams with noise.
- Ask AVA conversational troubleshooting
    - Engineers can interact with the network using natural, multi-turn conversations that follow their train of thought, dramatically reducing time to resolution.
- Continuous monitoring and automated root cause analysis
    - Issues are identified proactively, with clear explanations of what happened, why it happened, and what to do next.


<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  Why This Matters for Upstate NY Organizations
</p>

Whether you are supporting a school district, hospital, manufacturer, municipality, or enterprise campus, the challenges are the same. Limited staff, growing expectations, and no tolerance for downtime.

AVA was built to meet those realities head on. It was designed to help you move toward a network that is not just faster or bigger, but smarter, simpler, and easier to operate.

AVA is not searching the web for answers. It is learning from your network and working alongside your team.

Let’s see how AVA supercharges your environment.

## **Article #2 - What is a SerDes?**
By: Ryan Clark, Systems Engineer, Upstate NY Region

<div class="article-section" markdown>

When I first started working at Arista, I gained access to great minds in the field of layer-1 networking. If you think protocol acronyms are hard to keep track of - good luck in the hardware space…

One term I kept hearing over and over was **SerDes** (pronounced SIR-deez, rhymes with “birdies”). I kept thinking *“What the heck is this SerDes thing everyone keeps mentioning?”* 

Well, I am here to break it down for you. Thankfully timely due to Jeramiah’s article on AI (Artical #1). 

<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-style:italic; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  The Highway
</p>

Every network switch starts with a forwarding chip. For us, it’s typically manufactured by Broadcom. Each generation of these chips accepts an [encoded](https://www.keysight.com/blogs/en/inds/ai/pam4-pulse-amplitude-modulation-explained) **electrical signal** (1’s and 0’s) at a specific **speed** or **set of speeds**. 

Jericho2C+ (your 7280R3A) for example has 144 x 50G “lanes”. Think of these lanes as **lanes** on a highway and the **speed** is the MPH limit. Newer generations of switch silicon either increases the lanes or the MPH limit. This is commonly called the “**host-side**”.  

<figure markdown>
![Image Placement](img/Jan2026-SerDes-1.png){: style="height:350px;width:800px"} 
    <figcaption>  </figcaption>
</figure>

Some of these lanes are assigned to **front-panel ports** and some to **fabric ports**. The fabric ports internally stitch each front-panel port together so traffic can flow from one interface to another (green and yellow lines below). 

<figure markdown>
![Image Placement](img/Jan2026-SerDes-2.png){: style="height:425px;width:800px"} 
    <figcaption>  </figcaption>
</figure>


<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-style:italic; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  The Traffic Cops
</p>

Ok Ryan, I have a 7280CR3A-32S, but the interfaces I plug into are 100G, how does a chip with 50G SerDes do this? This is done with a **gearbox.** 

In the scope of this discussion, gearboxes provide **rate conversion**. In our example, the switch has two sets of gearboxes. One set converts (56) 50G SerDes **lanes** into (28) 100G front panel ports and another (16) 50G **lanes** into (4) 100G, (2) 200G or (1) 400G port. There is a lot more to it - but let's leave it there 😅. 

<figure markdown>
![Image Placement](img/Jan2026-SerDes-3.png){: style="height:375px;width:800px"} 
    <figcaption>  </figcaption>
</figure>


<p style="font-size:1.25em; font-style:italic; font-weight:bold; margin-top:1.2em; margin-bottom:0.4em;">
  The Offramps
</p>

We have now converted **electrical lanes** into a speed a transceiver can understand. We now need to chuck this signal from the traces (wires on the circuit board) inside the switch to a **transceiver** or **copper** cable. This is done with a **PHY**. A PHY handles the conversion and encoding necessary so the device on the other end can communicate with us ([1](https://www.arista.com/en/um-eos/eos-ethernet-ports#:~:text=The%20PHY%20provides%20translation%20services%20between%20the%20MAC%20layer%20and%20the%20transceiver)). 

The laser diode inside the transceiver converts the electrical signal into an optical signal or we carry the electrical signal over a copper cable - we commonly call that the “**line-side**”. And off we go!!

<figure markdown>
![Image Placement](img/Jan2026-SerDes-4.png){: style="height:150px;width:800px"} 
    <figcaption>  </figcaption>
</figure>

How does this relate to AI? Well, the Jericho3-AI chip in our new [7700R4 and 7800R4](https://www.arista.com/en/company/news/press-release/19493-arista-unveils-etherlink-ai-networking-platforms) switches has (**304**) **100G** SerDes for a total of **30Tb/s+** - from *one chip* (14.5 Tb/s line-side I/O). With a fully loaded 7800R4 supporting up to **460Tb/s** 🤯!!


</div>
